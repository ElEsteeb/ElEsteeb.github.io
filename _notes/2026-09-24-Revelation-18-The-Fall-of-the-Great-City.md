*“What the world calls great, heaven may have already pronounced fallen.”*

That was the thought I had in mind as I worked through Revelation 18. Revelation 17 spends a great deal of time showing us the woman and giving us clues about her identity. But Revelation 18 changes the question. It is no longer merely, “Who is this woman?” The chapter forces us to ask, “Why does she fall?”

It is very easy to turn Revelation into a puzzle book. We can spend all of our time trying to identify Babylon, the beast, the horns, the kings, and everything else John places before us. Some of that work is necessary. John expects us to understand what he is saying. But Revelation begins by promising a blessing not merely to the person who understands the prophecy, but to those who “hear, and who keep what is written in it” (Revelation 1:3).

So identifying Babylon cannot be the end of our work. We also need to understand why God judges her, and then ask the much more uncomfortable question of what we are supposed to keep from what we have read.

## Fallen, Fallen Is Babylon

Revelation 18 begins with an angel crying out, “Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!” (Revelation 18:2). John did not invent those words. Isaiah 21:9 announces the judgment of ancient Babylon with the same declaration: “Fallen, fallen is Babylon.”

That connection is direct. John wants us to hear Isaiah.

But John has already taught us something important about the way he uses the names of cities. In Revelation 11:8, the “great city” is called “Sodom and Egypt,” before John tells us that it is the place “where their Lord was crucified.”

Obviously, Sodom and Egypt are two different places, and Jesus was crucified in neither of them. John is not confused about geography. He is giving Jerusalem prophetic identities. Sodom was judged by God. Egypt was judged by God. Jerusalem had become like them, and Jerusalem would be judged by God.

Now another city enters the picture: Babylon. We should not rush ahead and identify her yet. Let Revelation 18 describe her first.

## “I Sit as a Queen”

A voice from heaven calls God's people out of Babylon: “Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins, lest you share in her plagues” (Revelation 18:4). Already, something interesting is happening. God's people are in this city, and they are being warned not to participate in her sins because her judgment is coming.

Then Babylon speaks for herself: “I sit as a queen, I am no widow, and mourning I shall never see” (Revelation 18:7). Once again, John is using the prophets. Isaiah describes ancient Babylon as one who said, “I shall be mistress forever,” who sat securely and said in her heart, “I am, and there is no one besides me; I shall not sit as a widow or know the loss of children” (Isaiah 47:7–8).

The resemblance is difficult to miss. What does Babylon believe about herself? She believes she is safe, secure, untouchable. “I will never mourn.” Yet Isaiah tells Babylon that her loss will come “in a moment, in one day” (Isaiah 47:9), and Revelation answers, “For this reason her plagues will come in a single day” (Revelation 18:8).

Babylon says, “I will never mourn.” God says her mourning is coming.

Her sin is not simply that she possesses wealth. Revelation will soon show us something much darker about her prosperity. But first we see the arrogance underneath it. Babylon has reached the point where she cannot imagine herself being judged. She sits as queen. Everything is fine. She is perfectly safe. Until suddenly she isn't.

## The World Does Not Love Babylon

When Babylon's judgment finally arrives, the kings of the earth begin to mourn. These are not strangers. Revelation says they “committed sexual immorality and lived in luxury with her” (Revelation 18:9). These are Babylon's allies.

But when they see her burning, they “stand far off.” Nobody rushes in to save Babylon. Her friends keep their distance and cry, “Alas! Alas! You great city, you mighty city, Babylon! For in a single hour your judgment has come” (Revelation 18:10).

Why are they mourning? Not because they have suddenly developed a concern for righteousness. They mourn because they have lost their partner in crime. Babylon was useful to them. They could sin with her. They could live in luxury with her. But when judgment comes, the kings who benefited from Babylon are nowhere near her.

Then John turns from the political world to the economic one. The merchants also weep and mourn. Why? Revelation tells us plainly: “since no one buys their cargo anymore” (Revelation 18:11).

Man, we lost our best customer.

John then gives us a long list of Babylon's merchandise: gold, silver, jewels, pearls, fine linen, purple cloth, silk, scarlet cloth, costly wood, ivory, bronze, iron, marble, spices, incense, wine, oil, flour, wheat, cattle, sheep, horses and chariots. Then the list reaches its terrible conclusion: human beings.

The merchants are not mourning Babylon because Babylon was good. They are mourning because Babylon was profitable. The world does not mourn Babylon because it loved her. The world mourns Babylon because it profited from her.

## Babylon Becomes Tyre

The cargo list should sound familiar to anyone who has spent time in Ezekiel 27. Ezekiel describes the enormous trading economy of Tyre with another long list of nations and merchandise. Silver, iron, tin, lead, horses, ivory, precious stones, purple cloth, fine linen, wheat, oil, wine, and human beings all appear in Ezekiel's description.

The lists are not identical, but the connection becomes even clearer when judgment falls. When Tyre falls, the sailors cry out. They mourn. They throw dust on their heads. They ask, “Who is like Tyre?” (Ezekiel 27:32). When Babylon falls, the sailors cry out. They mourn. They throw dust on their heads. They ask, “What city was like the great city?” (Revelation 18:18).

John is using Ezekiel's lament over Tyre to describe Babylon. This is important because John has already shown us how he uses cities symbolically. Jerusalem became Sodom and Egypt in Revelation 11. Now the great city is called Babylon while simultaneously being described with the prophetic language of Tyre.

The question is no longer simply, “Where is Babylon?” The better question is, “What has this city become?”

## When People Become Inventory

There is another reason the cargo lists matter. John could simply have said that Babylon was wealthy. Instead, he makes us walk through the merchandise until we reach the end of the list: people.

Babylon's prosperity has become inseparable from human exploitation. People have become inventory.

There is a possible connection here that I think is worth considering carefully. Near the end of Mark 12, Jesus condemns the scribes. Among His accusations is that they “devour widows' houses” (Mark 12:40).

Immediately afterward, Jesus sits across from the temple treasury and watches people putting money into it. Many rich people give large sums. Then a poor widow approaches and gives two small coins. Jesus points her out because the others gave out of their abundance, while she gave “everything she had, all she had to live on” (Mark 12:44).

Then Mark 13 begins. The disciples marvel at the temple. “Look, Teacher, what wonderful stones and what wonderful buildings!” Jesus responds that not one stone will be left upon another.

The widow's faith and sacrifice are striking, and I do not want to make the passage say more than it says. But given Mark's arrangement, we should at least ask whether the widow is also showing us what “devouring widows' houses” looks like. Jesus condemns religious leaders for devouring widows' houses. A widow gives everything she has to live on. The disciples marvel at the magnificent temple. Jesus announces its destruction.

That should bother us. God does not entrust weak people to His people so that His people can consume them. And Revelation 18 deliberately takes us through all the splendor of Babylon until its cargo list ends with human beings.

What the world sees as prosperity, heaven may see very differently.

## “Alas” on Earth, “Rejoice” in Heaven

The kings mourn. The merchants mourn. The sailors mourn. Again and again they cry, “Alas! Alas!”

Then something strange happens. Heaven does not join them.

> “Rejoice over her, O heaven, and you saints and apostles and prophets, for God has given judgment for you against her!” (Revelation 18:20)

Why does earth say “Alas” while heaven says “Rejoice”? What does each side see when it looks at Babylon?

The answer is contained in three small words: “For you.” God has given judgment for you. For the saints, for the apostles, for the prophets. This is not merely God judging a nation because nations should not do bad things. This judgment is also vindication.

Earth evaluates Babylon according to what it receives from her. Heaven evaluates Babylon according to what she has done to God's people. The kings see a partner. The merchants see a customer. The sailors see prosperity. God sees the people she exploited and the blood she shed.

That explains the strange difference between earth and heaven. The same judgment that causes the world to cry “Alas!” causes heaven to say “Rejoice!”

Jeremiah 51:48 gives us almost the same picture in the judgment of ancient Babylon: “Then the heavens and the earth, and all that is in them, shall sing for joy over Babylon.” God's judgment is vindication for those Babylon oppressed.

What the world calls great, heaven may have already pronounced fallen.

## “No More”

Then a mighty angel takes up a stone like a great millstone and throws it into the sea: “So will Babylon the great city be thrown down with violence, and will be found no more” (Revelation 18:21).

Again, John is drawing from Jeremiah. At the end of Jeremiah 51, a scroll containing God's judgment against Babylon is to be tied to a stone and thrown into the Euphrates: “Thus shall Babylon sink, to rise no more.”

Revelation continues by describing the silence that follows Babylon's destruction. There will be no more music, no more craftsmen, no more millstone, no more lamp, and no more bridegroom and bride. The great city goes silent.

Once again, John reaches into Jeremiah. Jeremiah 25:10 says, “Moreover, I will banish from them the voice of mirth and the voice of gladness, the voice of the bridegroom and the voice of the bride, the grinding of the millstones and the light of the lamp.”

But Jeremiah 25 is not describing God's judgment against Babylon. It is describing God's judgment against Judah and Jerusalem.

Now the prophetic identities are piling up: Sodom, Egypt, Babylon, Tyre, Jerusalem. John is tying them together.

But his final accusation makes the connection even stronger.

## The Blood of the Prophets

Revelation 18 ends with this accusation against Babylon: “And in her was found the blood of prophets and of saints, and of all who have been slain on earth” (Revelation 18:24).

That language should immediately remind us of Jesus' words in Matthew 23. Jesus tells the religious leaders, “Therefore I send you prophets and wise men and scribes, some of whom you will kill and crucify,” so that “on you may come all the righteous blood shed on earth” (Matthew 23:34–35).

Then He tells them when this judgment will come: “Truly, I say to you, all these things will come upon this generation” (Matthew 23:36). Immediately afterward He says, “O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it!” (Matthew 23:37).

Luke's account makes the parallel even more striking. Jesus says that God will send “prophets and apostles,” some of whom will be killed and persecuted, so that “the blood of all the prophets, shed from the foundation of the world,” may be charged against “this generation” (Luke 11:49–51).

Now place those passages beside Revelation 18. Matthew gives us prophets, persecution, righteous blood shed on earth, this generation, and Jerusalem. Luke gives us prophets and apostles, persecution, the blood of all the prophets, and this generation. Revelation gives us saints, apostles and prophets, their vindication, and the blood of prophets and saints found in Babylon.

I have no idea who John could possibly be talking about.

Jesus had already accused Jerusalem of killing God's messengers and declared that the accumulated blood of the righteous would be required of that generation. John makes essentially the same accusation against Babylon.

Babylon is Jerusalem.

But that conclusion becomes even more significant when we understand what John has done to get us there. Jerusalem has become everything she spent her history opposing. She has become Sodom. She has become Egypt. She has become Babylon. She has become Tyre. The people who were supposed to represent God before the nations had become like the nations God judged.

Judgment had come home.

## Babylon Says, Earth Says, Heaven Says, God Says

The entire chapter can almost be compressed into four voices. Babylon says, “I will never mourn.” Earth says, “Alas! Alas!” Heaven says, “Rejoice!” And God says, “No more.”

Babylon considered herself secure. The world considered her great because the world benefited from her. Heaven saw what the world did not, and God judged accordingly.

But if we stop there, we have only solved the puzzle.

Cool story, Steve. What does this have to do with me?

## See That You Do Not Refuse Him

Hebrews 12:25 says:

> “See that you do not refuse him who is speaking. For if they did not escape when they refused him who warned them on earth, much less will we escape if we reject him who warns from heaven.”

There are three levels that I think we need to keep separate when applying Revelation 18. First, there is the historical reference. I believe Revelation 18 is describing Jerusalem and the covenant judgment that culminated in AD 70. Jerusalem had become the very thing she was called to stand against. The people who were supposed to be a kingdom of priests and reveal God to the nations had become like the nations. God judged them.

Second, there is a biblical pattern. There is tremendous privilege in knowing God. Christianity is not built upon us feeling our way toward God and discovering whatever seems right to us. God speaks. God reveals Himself. He tells us who He is and what He requires.

That is an extraordinary privilege, but privilege does not create immunity. God sends warnings. He sent prophets. He sent wise men. He sent apostles. The warnings themselves are acts of mercy. But if God's warnings are continually refused, judgment follows.

Babylon's great error was believing that her position made judgment impossible. “I sit as queen. I am no widow. I will never mourn.” That is precisely the kind of security Scripture refuses to give to unfaithfulness.

And that brings us to the third level: the Christian warning. “See that you do not refuse him who is speaking.”

## Could We Become Babylon?

That is the question Revelation 18 eventually turns back on us. Could God's people become like Babylon? We have just spent an entire chapter looking at what happened when Jerusalem did.

So perhaps the first thing Revelation 18 should remove from us is presumption. Being God's people does not give us permission to exploit the people God has entrusted to us. We help the weak. We help the poor. We help the destitute. We do not turn people into merchandise.

We should never create a Christianity about which someone can say, “Man, I make a lot of money off those people.” The merchants loved Babylon right up until Babylon stopped being profitable. Then they stood far off.

Neither should the church confuse political usefulness with faithfulness. The kings enjoyed Babylon when Babylon benefited them. When judgment came, they stood far off too. The world will find God's people useful whenever association with us provides money, influence, legitimacy, votes, power, or anything else it desires. But usefulness is not faithfulness. Our question cannot be, “How valuable can we make ourselves to the powers around us?” Our question must be, “Are we faithful to Christ?”

There is another image in Revelation 18 that should at least remind us of something Christ has already said to His churches. Babylon's lamp goes dark. I do not think we need to make that a direct reference to Revelation 2, but Christ has already warned a church that unfaithfulness can result in its lampstand being removed. Having a lampstand does not guarantee having it forever. Christ's name is not a magical shield against Christ's discipline.

God cares about how His people live out the grace He has given them. It is not, “Well, you're saved. Go do whatever you want.” We are called to faithfulness.

Those of us who teach should feel that responsibility especially strongly. James warns that not many should become teachers because teachers “will be judged with greater strictness” (James 3:1). That is frightening, but it is also why we keep going back to what is written.

Our faith is not built on Steve standing in front of a room saying, “Guys, I know things.” God forbid. We have God's Word, and behind that Word is a faithful God.

So we should want to be faithful to Him.

## Do I See Babylon in Me?

Revelation 18 is about Jerusalem, but Revelation 18 was preserved for God's people. So it is not enough to finish the chapter saying, “Babylon is Jerusalem. I figured it out.”

Revelation began by promising a blessing to those who hear and keep what is written. So perhaps the final question is not merely, “Who is Babylon?” Perhaps it is, “Do I see Babylon in me?”

Do we trust our wealth? Do we trust our influence? Do we trust political alliances? Do we measure people according to what they can provide for us? Do we exploit the weak while admiring the institutions we have built? Do we mistake worldly usefulness for faithfulness? Do we assume that because God has blessed us, God must approve of everything we are doing? Do we hear Him when He warns us? Or have we become so secure in our identity that we can no longer imagine God correcting us?

Jerusalem had the temple. Jerusalem had the Scriptures. Jerusalem had the prophets. Jerusalem had the covenants. Jerusalem had privilege upon privilege. And none of those things made unfaithfulness safe.

Babylon says, “I will never mourn.” Earth says, “Alas!” Heaven says, “Rejoice!” God says, “No more.”

So when God speaks, we listen. When God warns, we repent. When He entrusts the weak to us, we protect them. When the world offers us power in exchange for faithfulness, we choose faithfulness.

Because what the world calls great, heaven may have already pronounced fallen.

And it is not enough for us to figure out whether Babylon is Jerusalem. We need the humility and wisdom to make sure that we do not see ourselves in her.
